Which denials are worth chasing? A four-question triage for practice owners

By F Cantor, Clearia · Published November 19, 2026 · Updated November 19, 2026

Chase a denial when you can win it, it's worth the time, and its deadline is still open. Four questions sort every group of claims into one of four actions: chase now, work normally, check first, or write off. Most of the money sits in patterns, not single claims, and in underpayments and downcoding that never show up as denials at all.

The money is worth sorting. In a 2024 survey by Premier, private insurers overturned more than 60 percent of the denials providers actually fought. Experian Health's 2025 survey found 41 percent of providers see denial rates of 10 percent or more. At one Miami orthopedic practice our team reviewed, 19% of insurance revenue was paid short or denied the first time.

So the problem isn't whether denials can be won. Most can. It's deciding which ones get your team's hours.

The four questions

  1. Can we win it? The reason is fixable on paper, the insurer has reversed it before, and the record supports the claim. If the insurer disputes medical necessity, it's a clinical appeal, a different job.
  2. Is it worth the hour? Count the expected recovery against the staff time. Premier put the cost of fighting one denial at about $44. A $30 denial worked by itself loses money.
  3. Is the insurer doing it again and again? One authorization denial may be a typo. Twelve from the same plan for the same code in six weeks is a rule firing. Fix the pattern once instead of twelve times.
  4. Will it happen next month? Find where it started: the front desk, coding, the visit note, or an insurer rule change. A denial you can trace is worth working even when it's small, because it stops the next one.

Start with fixable now vs. needs an appeal

Some denials are just waiting on something: a missing field, a record, an authorization number. Send it and they pay. Work these first, before they age into final denials with tighter deadlines. The rest are final, and they go through the four questions. Reading the code tells you which is which; our guide to denial codes and whether each is worth chasing covers the common ones.

Why patterns beat claims

One denial is a chore. Four hundred with the same reason from the same insurer are a single rule, and one fix pays for all of them. This is the math that makes small denials worth it: not one at a time, but grouped by insurer and reason. It's also why a write-off rule should come with a log. The claims you let go tell you where the next pattern is.

What the triage misses: money that never shows up as a denial

A triage only sorts what lands in the denial queue. Two of the three kinds of missing money never do. Underpayments are claims paid below your contract that post as paid. Downcoding is a visit paid at a lower level than you billed. Both close to a zero balance, so nobody looks. Finding them takes a line-by-line check against your contracts; see how to tell if an insurer is underpaying your practice and how payer downcoding works.

What this means for your practice

  • Ask for the write-off rule. Which denials does your team let go, and below what amount? It's question 5 in our list of questions to ask your billing company.
  • Count by pattern, not by claim. A monthly view by insurer and reason shows the few fixes worth most of the money.
  • Watch the clock. Most insurers allow 90 to 180 days. Check the Florida appeal deadlines by insurer before you sort by dollars.
  • Check what was paid, too. Underpayments and downcoding need a different check than denials.

Questions people ask

What makes a denial worth appealing?

Three things: you can win it (the fix is on paper and the insurer reverses that reason), it's worth the staff time once you count the hour it takes, and the appeal deadline is still open.

Should we appeal small denials?

One at a time, usually not. Grouped by insurer and reason, often yes: twenty small denials with the same cause can be fixed with one corrected process and one appeal strategy.

What's the difference between a soft and a hard denial?

A soft denial is waiting on something, such as a missing field, a record or an authorization number, and is fixed by sending it. A hard denial is a final no that needs a formal appeal. Soft denials turn hard if nobody works them.

How long do we have to appeal a denied claim?

It depends on the insurer and your contract. Most insurers allow 90 to 180 days, and once the window closes the money is gone, so deadlines decide the order of work.
